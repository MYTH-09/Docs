# 2. What Is Technical Communication?

The PDF defines technical communication as:

> **The exchange of information that helps people interact with technology and solve complex problems.**

This definition has two important parts:

- **Exchange of information** — communication happens between people.
- **Helping people interact with technology and solve problems** — the information has a practical purpose.

Technical communication is therefore much broader than just writing a report.

## Examples of technical communication

The PDF gives examples such as:

- Proposals
- Emails
- Reports
- Podcasts
- Computer help files
- Blogs
- Wikis
- Text messages
- Tweets
- Video conferences
- Presentations
- Instructions
- User manuals
- Procedures

So technical communication can happen in both **written and spoken** forms and in both **digital and physical** media.

### Simple example

Suppose a company develops a new printer.

Different technical communication may be required:

- A **design report** explains how the printer was developed.
- A **user manual** explains how customers operate it.
- A **maintenance procedure** tells technicians how to repair it.
- A **project proposal** asks management for funding.
- An **email** tells the team that testing is complete.
- A **presentation** explains the design to executives.

All of these are technical communication, but each one is written for a different purpose and audience.

# 4. Technical Communication vs. Academic Writing

One important distinction in the PDF is the difference between **academic writing** and **technical communication**.

## Academic writing

In typical college academic writing:

- The audience is often the **instructor**.
- The purpose is frequently to demonstrate that the student understands a topic.
- The student is often showing mastery of existing knowledge.

## Technical communication

In technical communication:

- The audience can be coworkers, supervisors, clients, users, managers, engineers, lawyers, or the public.
- The purpose is usually practical.
- The writer may need to:
  - change attitudes,
  - motivate an action,
  - help someone perform a task,
  - answer a question,
  - or help someone make a decision.

### Most important difference

The PDF emphasizes that technical communication has a different focus on:

- **Audience**
- **Purpose**

### Example

Imagine the same engineering research is written in three ways:

- A **research paper** for scientists
- A **press release** for the public
- A **blog post** for general readers

The underlying information may be similar, but the presentation changes because the audiences and purposes are different.

# 6. Internet Search Is Not the Same as Critical Thinking

The PDF says that simply searching the Internet is **not a substitute for critical thinking**.

When researching, you should ask:

- Is this information relevant to the situation?
- Is the source accurate?
- Can I verify the source?
- What does the information actually mean?
- What action does it suggest?
- How does it affect me or my colleagues?
- With whom should I share it?
- How might other people interpret it?

## Key idea

A search engine gives you information.

**Critical thinking tells you what to do with that information.**

### Example

Suppose you find an article claiming:

> “Battery X lasts twice as long as Battery Y.”

A technical communicator should not immediately use this statement.

They should ask:

- Who performed the test?
- What testing conditions were used?
- What does “twice as long” mean?
- Is the source independent?
- Is the result relevant to the current project?

This is how research becomes meaningful technical communication.

# 9. Reader-Centered Communication

A technical document should focus primarily on the **needs of the reader**.

Unlike poetry, fiction, or personal essays, technical documents are usually not centered on the writer's feelings and experiences.

The key question is:

> **What does the reader need to learn, do, or decide?**

## Reader-centered writing means:

Instead of asking:

> “What do I want to say?”

you should ask:

> “What does my reader need from this document?”

### Example

Suppose you are writing instructions for installing software.

A writer-centered approach might discuss:

> “I spent a lot of time testing this software...”

The reader-centered approach begins with:

> “Download the installer and run the setup program.”

The second version directly serves the reader.

---

# 10. Accessible and Efficient Communication

Readers expect to:

- Find the information they need
- Understand it quickly
- Get their questions answered

A good technical document should therefore reduce the amount of unnecessary effort required from the reader.

## Accessible writing

Accessible means the information can be understood and used by the intended audience.

For a nontechnical audience:

- Avoid unnecessary jargon.
- Use familiar words.
- Explain important technical terms.
- Use examples.
- Use diagrams where useful.

## Efficient writing

Efficient means the reader reaches the useful information quickly.

Instead of long blocks of technical text, use:

- Short paragraphs
- Headings
- Subheadings
- Lists
- Tables
- Diagrams
- Highlighted information

# 24. The Four Pillars of Effective Technical Communication

The PDF identifies four major pillars:

2. **Conciseness**
3. **Accuracy**
4. **Audience Focus**

---

# 25. Pillar 1 — Clarity

## Meaning

Clarity is the quality of being easy to understand.

The message should be **unmistakable**.

## How to achieve clarity

### 1. Use simple, direct language

Avoid difficult words when a simpler word works.

If technical jargon is necessary, define it.

### 2. Organize information logically

Use:

- Headings
- Subheadings
- Bullet points
- Numbered lists

### 3. Use strong, active verbs

Compare:

> “The motor was repaired by the technician.”

with:

> “The technician repaired the motor.”

The second version is more direct.

### 4. Use examples and analogies

When a concept is complex, connect it to something the reader already understands.

---

## Bad vs. good example

### Poor version

> “The project's synergistic functionalities necessitate a paradigm shift in our operational methodologies.”

### Better version

> “To complete this project, we need to change our workflow and collaborate more effectively.”

The second sentence is easier to understand because it uses ordinary words and states the action directly.

---

# 26. Pillar 2 — Conciseness

## Meaning

Conciseness means:

> Say what needs to be said in the fewest possible words **without losing clarity**.

Technical readers often have limited time.

## How to achieve conciseness

### Remove wordy phrases

Examples from the PDF:

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| Due to the fact that | Because |
| In order to | To |
| It is imperative that | You must |

### Remove redundancy

Do not repeat the same idea unnecessarily.

### Get to the point

The PDF recommends an **inverted pyramid** approach:

> Put the most important information first.

### Revise

After writing, remove unnecessary words and sentences.

---

## Wordy vs. concise example

### Wordy

> “In the event that the power supply happens to fail, it is a requirement that all users should save their work immediately and turn off their computers.”

### Concise

> “If the power fails, save your work and turn off your computer immediately.”

The concise version communicates the same instruction much more efficiently.

---

# 43. Phase 3 — Revising

Revision is **not the same as editing**.

This distinction is important.

The PDF describes revision as:

> **“To see again.”**

Revision looks at the **big picture**.

## What revision checks

### 1. Clarity

- Is the main point easy to find?
- Is the purpose clear?

### 2. Logical flow

- Is the information arranged logically?
- Do paragraphs connect smoothly?

### 3. Audience focus

- Is the detail appropriate?
- Is the tone suitable?

### 4. Completeness

- Has the document addressed all requirements?
- Is anything missing?

---

# 44. Effective Revision Methods

The PDF recommends three useful methods.

## Step away

Leave the document for a few hours or even a day.

Returning later can reveal problems that were invisible during continuous writing.

## Read aloud

Reading aloud can reveal:

- Awkward phrases
- Repetition
- Confusing sentences
- Poor transitions

## Seek feedback

Ask a peer to read the draft.

Another person may notice problems that the writer cannot see.

# 45. Revision Example from Glenn's Report

The PDF gives sentence-level examples to show how revision improves writing.

### Original

> “In my opinion, the Management Training Session for the month of October was somewhat successful. This success was evidenced when most participants rated their training as ‘very good.’”

Problems:

- “In my opinion” is unnecessary.
- “for the month of October” is wordy.
- “somewhat successful” sounds weak.
- The two sentences are repetitive.
- The wording is less direct.

### Revised

> “The October Management Training Session was successful, with training rated ‘very good’ by most participants.”

This version is:

- Shorter
- More direct
- More professional
- Less repetitive

---

# 46. Revision Should Also Improve Tone

The original sentence:

> “But improvements are still needed.”

is very blunt.

The revised idea is more diplomatic:

> “A few changes—beyond the recent innovations—should result in even greater training efficiency.”

The point is still communicated, but the tone is more constructive.

This is especially important in workplace communication because the writer should avoid unnecessarily alienating the reader.

---

# 47. Phase 4 — Editing

Editing occurs after the structure and content have been improved.

Editing is:

> **A line-by-line check for errors.**

Revision asks:

> “Is the document structurally effective?”

Editing asks:

> “Are the details correct and polished?”

---

# 48. What to Check During Editing

## Grammar and punctuation

Check:

- Subject-verb agreement
- Commas
- Sentence structure
- Other grammar issues

## Spelling

Check for:

- Typos
- Misspelled words

Spell-check helps but is not enough.

## Word choice

Replace:

- Wordy expressions
- Imprecise words
- Inappropriate technical terms

Example:

> “in order to” → “to”

## Formatting

Check consistency in:

- Headings
- Lists
- Citations
- Figures
- Tables

Make sure figures and tables are correctly labeled and referenced.

## Numbers and units

Check:

- Numerical accuracy
- Units
- Consistency

The PDF gives examples such as:

- MPa
- V
- °C

---

# 49. The Four Phases — Easy Way to Remember

Think of building a technical document like building an engineering system.

| Phase | Main question | Main job |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | What should I build? | Define audience, purpose, scope, format |
| Drafting | Can I get the ideas down? | Generate content |
| Revising | Does the whole thing work? | Improve structure, flow, completeness, audience fit |
| Editing | Are the details correct? | Fix grammar, spelling, formatting, numbers |

### Critical distinction

**Revision = big picture**

**Editing = small details**

# 50. Audience and Purpose Analysis

The PDF next focuses heavily on:

> **Audience and Purpose Analysis**

The core principle is:

> Before writing, understand **who** will use the document, **why** they will use it, and **how** they will use it.

---

# 51. Questions to Ask During Audience Analysis

Before writing, ask:

### Who?

- Who is the main audience?
- Who else may read it?
- What is my relationship with the audience?
- Are there multiple types of relationships?

### What?

- What information does the audience need?
- How familiar are they with the technical material?
- Do readers have different levels of expertise?

### Culture

- What culture or cultures does the audience represent?
- Could cultural differences affect expectations or interpretation?

### Medium

Will the document be viewed:

- On a computer?
- On a phone?
- On a tablet?
- On paper?

The medium can affect the way the document should be designed.

---

# 52. What Is Audience and Purpose Analysis?

The PDF defines it as the process of:

### Audience Analysis

Identifying readers':

- Characteristics
- Needs
- Expectations

### Purpose Analysis

Defining the goal of the document.

Doing both helps ensure the document is:

- Clear
- Relevant
- Effective

---

# 53. Why Audience and Purpose Analysis Matters

Engineers regularly prepare:

- Project proposals
- Progress reports
- User manuals
- Research papers

Without audience and purpose analysis, a document may be:

- Misunderstood
- Ignored
- Ineffective
- Unsuitable for the situation

Therefore:

> A technically correct document can still fail if it is written for the wrong audience or purpose.

---

# 54. Three Tiers of Audience

The PDF categorizes audiences into three groups:

1. **Primary audience**
2. **Secondary audience**
3. **Tertiary audience**

These categories help writers understand different readers.

---

# 55. Primary Audience

The **primary audience** is the main target audience.

They are the direct recipients and are usually the people who will use the document to:

- Make a decision
- Take action
- Perform a task

## Examples

- Project manager deciding on a proposal
- End-user operating a machine
- Client reviewing a report

## What they need

They need:

- Direct information
- Actionable information
- Appropriate detail
- Minimal unnecessary jargon

## Goal

Make their job easier and help them achieve their objective.

---

# 56. Secondary Audience

The **secondary audience** consists of reviewers, advisors, or people who evaluate the document.

They may not directly use the document to take the main action.

## Examples

- Professor grading a lab report
- Legal team reviewing a contract
- Senior engineer checking specifications

## What they need

They often look for:

- Accuracy
- Detailed data
- Methods
- Logical organization
- Compliance
- Quality

## Goal

Provide enough evidence for the document to withstand scrutiny.

---

# 57. Tertiary Audience

The **tertiary audience** is the broader or more distant group.

They may use the document later for:

- Reference
- Archival purposes
- Historical understanding

## Examples

- Future employees
- Auditors
- Researchers
- General public

## What they need

They benefit from:

- Clear headings
- Good indexing
- Table of contents
- Easy searching
- Appendices
- Good archival structure

## Goal

Make the document useful over the long term.

---

# 58. Primary vs. Secondary Audience

The PDF also emphasizes a practical distinction between primary and secondary audiences.

### Primary readers

Often include decision makers who requested the document.

### Secondary readers

May:

- Carry out the project
- Advise decision makers
- Be affected by the decision
- Review compliance or quality

### Example

Suppose you prepare installation instructions for office email software.

- **Primary audience:** Computer support staff who will install it.
- **Secondary audience:** Managers checking company-policy compliance and lawyers checking legal requirements.

This shows that one document may need to satisfy more than one audience.

# 60. The Three Questions to Ask Before Writing

The PDF gives a useful takeaway.

### Primary audience

> Who is my primary audience, and what do they need to know to take action?

### Secondary audience

> Who is my secondary audience, and what detailed information do they need to verify my work?

### Tertiary audience

> Who is my tertiary audience, and how can I make the document useful as a long-term resource?

These questions should be answered before writing.

# 61. Analyzing Audience Needs — “What Do They Need to Know?”

The audience is usually reading because they have:

- A problem
- A question
- A decision to make
- A task to perform

The writer should provide:

> **Exactly what the reader needs — no more, no less.**

---

# 62. The “So What?” Principle

Do not merely provide facts.

Explain their **significance**.

### Example from the PDF

Weak:

> “The tensile strength is 500 MPa.”

Better:

> “The material's high tensile strength of 500 MPa makes it suitable for high-stress aerospace applications.”

The second version answers:

> “Why does this number matter?”

### Important lesson

Technical communication should connect:

**Fact → Meaning → Consequence**

---

# 63. Action-Oriented Content

Every document should make clear what the reader should do next.

### User manual

The reader needs to:

> Operate a machine.

### Proposal

The reader needs to:

> Approve funding.

### Report

The reader needs to:

> Understand results and make a decision.

A useful self-check is:

> Does every section support the purpose of the document?

---

# 64. Assessing Audience Knowledge

Writers must determine how much the audience already knows.

The PDF compares explaining a circuit to:

- A first-year student
- A senior professor

Clearly, the explanation would be different.

This affects:

- Writing style
- Jargon
- Equations
- Acronyms
- Background information
- Level of detail

---

# 65. Expert Audience

An expert audience consists of specialists who already know the field.

The writer may:

- Use technical terminology
- Use equations
- Use acronyms
- Discuss detailed methodology

### Example

A research paper for a specialized engineering journal.

The reader expects technical detail.

---

# 66. Novice Audience

A novice has little or no knowledge of the subject.

The writer should:

- Avoid unnecessary jargon
- Explain important technical terms
- Use simple language
- Use analogies
- Focus on the big picture
- Explain practical implications

### Example

A user manual for a consumer device.

---

# 67. Mixed Audience

A mixed audience includes both experts and non-experts.

This is common in real workplaces.

A useful strategy is:

1. Begin with a summary or introduction understandable to everyone.
2. Move into more detailed technical material.
3. Use appendices for additional detail.
4. Use glossaries when terminology requires explanation.

This allows different readers to use different parts of the same document.

---

# 68. Managing Audience Expectations

Readers come to a document with expectations about:

- Content
- Structure
- Tone

Meeting those expectations builds:

- Trust
- Readability
- Ease of navigation

---

# 69. Structure and Organization

Use a predictable structure.

For example:

1. Introduction
2. Background
3. Methodology
4. Results
5. Conclusion

Use headings and subheadings to:

- Divide information
- Guide the reader
- Show what each section contains

A good heading should summarize the section.

---

# 70. Visuals and Audience Expectations

Tables, graphs, and diagrams should be:

- Clear
- Properly labeled
- Directly relevant

A visual should not be included merely for decoration.

Its purpose should be to help the reader understand information more efficiently.

---

# 71. Tone and Formality

The PDF explains that tone should match the situation.

## Formal tone

Appropriate for situations such as:

- Client reports
- Formal business communication
- Academic communication

It uses:

- Professional language
- Precise wording
- No unnecessary slang

## Semiformal tone

May be appropriate for:

- Internal memos
- Workplace announcements
- Professional communication between colleagues

## Informal tone

May be appropriate for:

- Quick internal messages
- Familiar colleagues
- Casual workplace situations

But even informal writing should remain professional.

---

# 72. Completeness

A technical document should answer the important questions readers have.

Check:

- Have all important questions been answered?
- Is necessary data included?
- Are references included?
- Are citations included where needed?

A document that is accurate but incomplete can still fail its purpose.

---

# 73. Three Primary Purposes of Technical Communication

The PDF says most forms of technical communication serve one or more of three major purposes:

1. **Inform**
2. **Instruct**
3. **Persuade**

These purposes can overlap.

---

# 74. Purpose 1 — Inform

Informational communication is designed to:

> Answer readers' questions clearly and efficiently.

The goal is mainly to provide useful understanding.

### Example

A document explaining:

- What a technology does
- What its benefits are
- What its risks are
- How a process works

may primarily inform.

The PDF's thermostat example is primarily informational in part because it provides information for a broad audience that may have questions but little technical knowledge.

---

# 75. Purpose 2 — Instruct

Instructional communication helps people:

> **Do something.**

Examples:

- Assemble a computer
- Perform CPR
- Operate a machine
- Configure software
- Follow a safety procedure

Instructional writing should generally use:

- Direct language
- Clear steps
- Action verbs
- Helpful visuals

The thermostat example uses phrases such as:

- “Think about your schedule.”
- “Decide which model best fits.”

These are direct and action-oriented.

---

# 76. Purpose 3 — Persuade

Persuasive communication encourages the reader to:

> **Take a desired action or adopt a viewpoint.**

Examples:

- Sales letters
- Project proposals
- Funding requests
- Recommendations

Technical documents may be persuasive even when they look highly technical.

### Example

A technical document may explain energy savings to encourage people to adopt a programmable thermostat.

So technical writing can contain persuasion without becoming advertising.

---

# 77. Inform + Instruct + Persuade Can Overlap

A single document may serve several purposes.

For example, a safety manual may:

- **Inform** users about a hazard.
- **Instruct** them on what to do.
- **Persuade** them to follow the procedure.

Therefore, the purposes are not always separate.

---

# 78. Tone — What Is It?

The PDF explains tone as the image of the writer's personality that comes through the writing.

Tone communicates:

- Relationship with the reader
- Attitude toward the subject
- Level of formality

Tone can range from:

**Formal → Semiformal → Informal**

---

# 79. Tone and the Workplace

Workplace readers expect tone to match:

- Importance of the topic
- Urgency
- Relationship between writer and reader

### Example

A formal letter to a client may appropriately say:

> “We are pleased to forward your annual investment statement.”

But the same type of wording may sound stiff in a casual internal announcement.

So:

> The same tone is not suitable for every situation.

---

# 80. Tone Can Also Range from Friendly to Hostile

Tone is not only about formality.

It can also range from:

- Friendly
- Encouraging
- Neutral
- Distant
- Hostile

A bossy tone can make employees feel:

- Demeaned
- Resentful
- Disrespected

The PDF recommends a tone that sounds like a professional and likable person talking to people in a workplace setting.

---

# 81. Adjusting Tone

The tone depends on two things emphasized in the PDF:

1. **The distance between writer and reader**
2. **The writer's attitude toward the subject**

### Example

The same job instructions can be written in three different tones.

#### Informal

> “Now that you've arrived in the glamorous world of office work, put on your running shoes...”

This creates very little distance and uses humor.

#### Semiformal

> “As a manager trainee at GlobalTech, you will work for many managers. In short, you will spend little of your day seated at your desk.”

This sounds serious but still directly addresses trainees.

#### Formal

> “Manager trainees at GlobalTech are responsible for duties that extend far beyond desk work.”

This is more impersonal and businesslike.

### Lesson

The content can stay similar while the tone changes dramatically.

---

# 82. Tone Announces Interpersonal Distance

The PDF gives a sequence of greetings to demonstrate tone:

- “Honored to make your acquaintance.” → formal
- “How do you do?” → formal
- “Nice to meet you.” → semiformal
- “Hello.” → semiformal
- “Hi.” → informal
- “What’s happening?” → informal/slang

The meaning is similar, but the relationship implied by each greeting is different.

Therefore:

> **Tone announces interpersonal distance.**

---

# 83. Be Consistent with Tone

Once you choose a tone, keep it consistent.

The PDF contrasts:

### Too informal

> “My office isn’t fit for a pig.”

### Too formal

> “It is ungraciously unattractive.”

### More appropriate

> “My office is so shabby that it’s an awful place to work.”

In general, aim for:

> **Professional yet friendly**

---

# 84. Tone Also Communicates Attitude

Consider these different ways of saying essentially the same thing:

- We dine at seven.
- Dinner is at seven.
- Let's eat at seven.
- Let's chow down at seven.
- Let's pig out at seven.

The word choice communicates the writer's attitude and level of formality.

Therefore, word choice is part of tone.

---

# 85. Guidelines for Choosing Tone

The PDF provides these general guidelines:

## For superiors, professionals, academics

Use:

- Formal
- Semiformal

depending on expectations.

## For colleagues and subordinates

Use:

- Semiformal
- Informal

depending on relationship.

## For conversational writing

An informal tone can work, but it should still be professional.

## When giving unpleasant information

Avoid a negative tone.

Instead, emphasize constructive improvements where appropriate.

## When uncertain

Do not be too casual.

---

# 86. Contractions

The PDF discusses contractions such as:

- I am → I'm
- You are → you're
- It is → it's

Contractions can make writing sound more natural.

However:

> Contractions are rarely appropriate in highly formal business writing.

Also, the uncontracted form may sound more emphatic.

### Example

> “Don't handle this material without protective clothing.”

vs.

> “Do not handle this material without protective clothing.”

The second can sound stronger and more formal.

---

# 87. Address Readers Directly

The PDF recommends using:

- **you**
- **your**

when appropriate.

Direct address often makes communication feel more connected to the reader.

### Impersonal

> “Students at this college will find the faculty always willing to help.”

### Personal

> “As a student at this college, you will find the faculty always willing to help.”

The second directly connects with the reader.

### Important caution

Do not use “you” automatically.

It is most suitable when the document is directly addressing a reader, such as:

- Letters
- Memos
- Instructions

In descriptions or narration, unnecessary use of “you” can become awkward.

---

# 88. Use I and We When Appropriate

Do not make the writer or organization disappear when first-person references are useful.

### Impersonal

> “The writer of this letter would like a refund.”

### Personal

> “I would like a refund.”

Likewise:

### Doubly impersonal

> “The requested report will be sent next week.”

### More direct

> “We will send the report you requested next week.”

The PDF's point is that technical writing can remain professional without unnecessarily hiding the people responsible for the communication.

---

# 89. Prefer Active Voice

The PDF recommends active voice because it is generally:

- More direct
- More economical
- Less formal

### Passive

> “The motor was repaired by the technician.”

### Active

> “The technician repaired the motor.”

Active voice often makes responsibility clearer.

---

# 90. Emphasize the Positive

When giving advice or recommendations, emphasize benefits instead of simply pointing out flaws.

### Critical version

> “Because of your division's lagging productivity, a management review may be needed.”

### Encouraging version

> “A management review might help boost productivity in your division.”

The second communicates the same concern but is more constructive.

---

# 91. Avoid an Overly Informal Tone

A conversational tone does **not** mean writing exactly the way you speak with friends.

Avoid:

- Slang
- Profanity
- Poor grammar
- Excessive colloquialisms

Examples of inappropriate casual language include slang such as:

- “bogus”
- “bummed”

and informal spoken forms such as:

- “He ain't got none.”
- “I seen it today.”

Such language is generally inappropriate in professional workplace writing.

---

# 92. When Does Tone Become Offensive?

Tone can offend when it violates the reader's expectations.

A tone may seem:

- Disrespectful
- Tasteless
- Too distant
- Aloof
- Too “chummy”
- Too casual

The same words can therefore be acceptable in one situation and inappropriate in another.

Context matters.

---

# 93. Academic and Formal Tone

The PDF notes that formal or academic tone is appropriate for situations such as:

- Research papers
- Job applications
- Reports for senior executives

For example, a formal academic essay should not use childish slang or overly casual expressions.

The PDF also notes that rough drafting can naturally produce an overly informal tone. That is acceptable during brainstorming and drafting because tone can be adjusted during revision.

---

# 94. Avoid Personal Bias

An objective technical document should avoid inserting unnecessary personal bias.

The PDF gives an example involving an employee-management confrontation.

A factual description might simply state:

- What happened
- When it happened
- How many people were involved
- What they claimed

without emotionally loaded judgments.

---

# 95. Factual vs. Biased Language

### Factual style

A factual account reports observable information and attributes claims appropriately.

### Biased style

A biased account uses emotionally loaded words to tell the reader how to feel.

The PDF points out that words such as:

- “male supremacy”
- “repressive”
- “degrading”
- “paralyzed”
- “articulate”

can inject the writer's judgment.

A different biased version can also unfairly defend the opposite side using words such as:

- “amusing”
- “irate”
- “alleged”

The lesson is:

> When the purpose is objective reporting, describe the facts without unnecessary judgment.

---

# 96. Why Bias Is a Technical Writing Problem

Bias can reduce:

- Credibility
- Fairness
- Trust
- Professionalism

If readers believe that the writer is manipulating the description, they may stop trusting the document.

Therefore, objective wording is especially important in:

- Reports
- Investigations
- Research
- Technical evaluations

---

# 97. Avoid Sexist Usage

The PDF also discusses language that makes unfair gender assumptions.

Avoid automatically using:

- “he” for doctors and engineers
- “she” for nurses or secretaries

Likewise, some occupational terms may unnecessarily imply that only one gender performs the job.

Examples:

- Foreman → Supervisor
- Fireman → Firefighter

The goal is more inclusive language.

---

# 98. Guidelines for Nonsexist Usage

The PDF suggests several strategies.

## Use neutral expressions

Examples:

- Chairman → Chair / Chairperson
- Postman → Postal worker

## Rephrase sentences

Instead of:

> “A writer will succeed if he revises.”

Use:

> “A writer who revises succeeds.”

## Use plural forms

For example:

> “Writers will succeed if they revise.”

This can avoid unnecessary gendering.

## Use paired pronouns when necessary

For example:

> “A writer will succeed if she or he revises.”

The PDF also recommends avoiding condescending diminutive endings where they are being used to mark females.

---

# 99. Professional Titles

The PDF notes that:

> “Ms.” can be used instead of “Mrs.” or “Miss” unless a person's preference is known.

Titles can also sometimes be omitted:

- Roger Smith
- Jane Kelly

rather than using titles that add unnecessary assumptions.

---

# 100. Handling Sexist Language in a Quoted Source

When a source uses outdated or sexist language, the PDF gives options such as:

- Insert `[sic]` after the problematic wording
- Use ellipses to omit it
- Paraphrase instead of quoting
- Replace or insert nonsexist wording using brackets when appropriate

The main principle is to preserve accuracy while avoiding unnecessary reproduction of biased wording.

---

# 101. Adapting Style for Different Contexts

The PDF later broadens the discussion from tone to **style**.

It emphasizes:

> No matter how technical the document is, readers will not understand it unless the style is readable.

Style includes:

- Sentence construction
- Sentence length
- Connections between sentences
- Word choice
- Tone

Therefore, technical content alone is not enough.

---

# 102. Readable Style

Readable style requires:

- Correct grammar
- Correct punctuation
- Correct spelling

But:

> **Correctness alone does not guarantee readability.**

A document can be grammatically correct and still be very difficult to read.

---

# 103. Example of Mechanically Correct but Inefficient Writing

The PDF gives a formal job-application response containing phrases such as:

- “We are in receipt of your recent correspondence...”
- “Your correspondence has been duly forwarded...”
- “You may expect to hear from us...”

The problem is not necessarily grammar.

The problem is that the reader has to work too hard to understand a simple message.

A clearer version communicates the same idea much more simply:

> The application has been forwarded to the personnel office. We will be in touch as the selection process moves forward. Thank you for your interest.

The important lesson:

> **Readers should not have to struggle to extract simple information.**

---

# 104. Why Inefficient Style Is a Problem

The PDF identifies several causes of inefficient style.

A style may:

- Be difficult or impossible to interpret
- Take too long to reach the point
- Sound childish
- Use imprecise or unnecessarily large words
- Sound stiff and impersonal

The result is writing that is:

- Less informative
- Less persuasive
- Less useful

---

# 105. Inefficient Style Can Also Be Unethical

This is an important point from the PDF.

If poor style:

- Confuses readers
- Misleads readers
- Hides important meaning

then it can become an ethical problem.

This can happen intentionally or unintentionally.

Therefore, the writer should spend more time revising so the reader can spend less time struggling.

The PDF describes an ideal style as:

- Clear
- Concise
- Fluent
- Exact
- Likable

---

# 106. Preparing Effective Technical Documents

The final section asks a central question:

> **How do I prepare the right document for this group of readers and this particular situation?**

The PDF presents four tasks necessary for an effective document.

The final figure on **page 104** shows these four tasks around an effective document:

1. **Information Delivery**
2. **Persuasive Reasoning**
3. **Ethical Presentation**
4. **Good Teamwork**

The four tasks support the central goal:

> **An Effective Document**

---

# 107. Task 1 — Information Delivery

The writer must deliver information that readers can actually use.

Why?

Because:

> Different people in different situations have different information needs.

This connects directly to audience analysis.

### Example

A CEO may need:

- Costs
- Risks
- Benefits
- Business impact

A technician may need:

- Detailed procedures
- Technical specifications
- Measurements
- Troubleshooting steps

Same project. Different information needs.

---

# 108. Task 2 — Persuasive Reasoning

People do not always agree about:

- What information means
- What should happen next
- Which action is best

Technical communicators therefore sometimes need to use persuasive reasoning.

This does not mean manipulating the reader.

It means:

- Present evidence
- Explain its meaning
- Connect evidence to conclusions
- Justify recommendations

---

# 109. Task 3 — Ethical Presentation

The PDF states that ethical issues must be considered because unethical communication:

- Lacks credibility
- Can alienate readers

Ethical technical communication should therefore avoid:

- Deliberate deception
- Misleading presentation
- Unnecessary bias
- Unfair language
- Distortion of information

A technical document should represent information responsibly.

---

# 110. Task 4 — Good Teamwork

Technical documents are often created by teams.

The PDF connects this to the reality that a large proportion of workers spend some part of their day working in teams.

Good technical teamwork requires people to:

- Share information
- Communicate clearly
- Coordinate work
- Review each other's material
- Resolve disagreements
- Maintain consistency

# 112. Important Distinctions to Memorize

## Technical communication vs. technical writing

### Technical communication

Broad concept involving the exchange of technical information through:

- Writing
- Speaking
- Reading
- Listening
- Digital communication
- Presentations
- Documents
- Other media

### Technical writing

A specific process of documenting and explaining complex technical information clearly, concisely, and accurately for a specific audience.

So:

> **Technical writing is part of technical communication.**

# 113. The Four Pillars vs. Four Process Phases

Do not confuse these two groups.

## Four pillars

2. Conciseness
3. Accuracy

## Four process phases

1. Planning
2. Drafting
3. Revising
4. Editing

### Easy memory trick

**Pillars = What good writing should be**

**Process = How you produce it**

| Expert | Technical terminology, equations, detailed methodology |
| Novice | Simple language, explanations, analogies, practical focus |
| Mixed | Accessible introduction + detailed technical sections + appendices/glossary |

---

# 116. Inform, Instruct, Persuade — Quick Comparison

| Purpose | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inform | Give useful understanding | Explain how a system works |
| Instruct | Help someone perform a task | Equipment instructions |
| Persuade | Encourage an action or decision | Project proposal |

Remember that one document can have more than one of these purposes.

---

# 117. Tone — Quick Comparison

| Tone | Typical context | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | Clients, academics, senior officials | Professional, precise, distant |
| Semiformal | Workplace colleagues/memos | Professional, direct |
| Informal | Familiar colleagues | Conversational but still professional |

The correct tone depends on the reader and situation.

# 118. Revision vs. Editing — Very Important

### Revision

Looks at:

- Main idea
- Organization
- Flow
- Audience
- Purpose
- Completeness
- Tone

Think:

> **“Does the document work?”**

### Editing

Looks at:

- Grammar
- Spelling
- Punctuation
- Word choice
- Formatting
- Numbers
- Units

Think:

> **“Are the details correct?”**

A very useful question for the entire unit is:

This question can guide:

- Audience analysis
- Purpose
- Organization
- Tone
- Level of detail
- Visual design
- Word choice

# 120. Exam-Oriented Definitions

These are the major definitions/concepts from the supplied PDF.

## Technical communication

The exchange of information that helps people interact with technology and solve complex problems.

## Technical writing

The process of documenting, explaining, and communicating complex technical information clearly, concisely, and accurately to a specific audience.

## Clarity

The quality of being easy to understand.

## Conciseness

Communicating what needs to be said in the fewest possible words without sacrificing clarity.

## Accuracy

The quality of being correct and free from errors.

## Audience focus

Tailoring communication to the knowledge level, needs, and expectations of the reader.

## Primary audience

The main target readers who directly use the document to make a decision or take action.

## Secondary audience

Reviewers, advisors, or other readers who evaluate the document for accuracy, compliance, or quality.

## Tertiary audience

Broader or later readers who may use the document for reference or archival purposes.

## Technical communication purposes

To inform, instruct, and persuade.

## Revision

Re-seeing the document at the big-picture level to improve clarity, flow, audience focus, and completeness.

## Editing

A detailed, line-by-line check for correctness and consistency.

# 121. Important “Why?” Questions

These are useful for understanding rather than memorizing.

## Why should technical writing be reader-centered?

Because the document exists to help the reader learn, act, or decide.

## Why is clarity important?

Because misunderstanding technical information can produce project, business, or safety problems.

## Why is conciseness important?

Because readers have limited time and should not have to work through unnecessary words.

## Why is accuracy critical?

Because technical mistakes can damage equipment, cause safety problems, reduce credibility, or produce incorrect decisions.

## Why analyze the audience?

Because different readers need different information, terminology, detail, tone, and formats.

## Why revise after drafting?

Because a writer usually cannot see every structural or communication problem while producing the first draft.

## Why edit after revising?

Because once the large structure is correct, the writer can focus on correcting details.

## Why avoid unnecessary bias?

Because biased language reduces objectivity and credibility.

## Why is tone important?

Because tone communicates the relationship between writer and reader and the writer's attitude toward the subject.

# 123. Final Takeaway

The most important message of the PDF is that technical communication is **purpose-driven and reader-centered**.

A strong technical communicator does not simply know technical information.

They know how to:

1. Understand the communication situation.
2. Identify the audience.
3. Determine the audience's knowledge and needs.
4. Define the purpose.
5. Select the appropriate format.
6. Plan the document.
7. Draft the content.
8. Revise the big picture.
9. Edit the details.
10. Present information clearly, concisely, accurately, ethically, and professionally.

The goal is not to make writing sound complicated.

The goal is to make **complex information usable**.
