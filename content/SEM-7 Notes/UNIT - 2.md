### **1. The Research Process & Critical Thinking**

#### **Procedural Stages vs. Critical Thinking**

- **Procedural Sequence**: Workplace research follows a procedural sequence consisting of four main stages: searching for information, recording findings, documenting sources, and writing the final document.
- **Critical Thinking Integration**: Research is not merely a mechanical set of procedures; it requires continuous critical thinking at every step.
- **Five Stages of Critical Thinking**:
    1. **Asking the right questions**.
    2. **Exploring a balance of views**.
    3. **Achieving adequate depth in your search**.
    4. **Evaluating your findings**.
    5. **Interpreting your findings**.

#### **Defining the Research Problem**

- **Refining Questions**: The quality of research answers depends directly on the quality and specificity of the initial research questions.
- **Subordinate Questions & Tree Diagrams**: Complex research problems must be broken down into major and subordinate questions using tree diagrams to define the full scope of inquiry.

#### **Exploring a Balance of Views**

- **Multiple Angles**: Researchers must approach topics from diverse perspectives rather than settling for comforting or convenient answers.
- **Evaluating Agreement & Disagreement**: Examine where reputable sources agree and where they conflict.
- **Balance vs. Accuracy**: "Balance" means sampling a full spectrum of opinions, whereas "accuracy" means arriving at objective facts. Not all sources carry equal weight, and biased claims should not be reported as objective facts.

#### **Search Depth Levels**

- **Level 1 (Surface Level)**: Popular media (newspapers, general magazines), which only skim the surface of complex issues.
- **Level 2 (Deeper Level)**: Trade, business, and technical publications (e.g., _Publisher's Weekly_), focusing on industry practice, practical field issues, and public relations for moderately to highly specialized readers.
- **Level 3 (Deepest Level)**: Specialized literature (peer-reviewed academic, medical, legal, and engineering journals) written by researchers focusing on theory, cutting-edge studies, scholarly debates, and rigorous reviews. This level also includes government documents and corporate files obtained via Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests.

### **2. Primary vs. Secondary Research & Source Types**

#### **Primary Research**

- **Definition**: Collecting new, original data directly from first-hand sources to expand existing knowledge and enhance credibility.
- **Key Methods**:
    - **Informational Interviews**: Gathering expert opinions across a balanced range of stakeholders (e.g., company engineers, environmentalists, and neutral academic experts).
    - **Surveys & Questionnaires**: Using clear cover emails to state the project purpose, maintaining a polite tone, and designing clean layouts with both open-ended (qualitative) and closed-ended (quantitative) questions.
    - **Experiments & Testing**: Conducting physical tests and logging observational data.

#### **Secondary Research**

- **Definition**: Investigating already published material to build background understanding before conducting primary research.
- **Online Secondary Sources**:
    - **Search Engines & Subject Directories**: Subject directories compiled by editors (e.g., About.com) and search engines like Google help brainstorm and locate web pages.
    - **Wikipedia**: Useful strictly as a starting point to gain a broad topic overview and locate references/footnotes, but not as a final authoritative source.
    - **Social Media & Feeds**: Corporate Twitter feeds and Facebook pages can provide leads to recent studies or public advisory notices.
    - **Ethical Web Usage**: Download only necessary files, respect copyright, and obtain written permission for commercial use.
- **Traditional Hard-Copy Sources**:
    - **Books & Periodicals**: Provide thorough coverage; always check copyright dates for currency.
    - **Reference Works & Bibliographies**: General directories like _Bibliographic Index Plus_ or subject-specific bibliographies.
    - **Indexes**: Technical report indexes, newspaper indexes, periodical indexes (e.g., _Readers' Guide_), citation indexes (tracking where original material is quoted), and patent indexes (e.g., USPTO).
    - **Encyclopedias, Dictionaries & Handbooks**: General and field-specific reference manuals (e.g., _Civil Engineering Handbook_) providing quick formulas, definitions, and data tables.
    - **Abstracts**: Collected summaries organized by discipline (e.g., _Biological Abstracts_, _Dissertation Abstracts International_) to evaluate source relevance quickly.
- **Gray Literature**: Unpublished or uncataloged materials not found in standard library catalogs, such as corporate pamphlets, unpublished government documents, student dissertations, and conference proceedings. Because gray literature lacks formal editorial peer review, it must be cross-verified with other sources.

### **3. Skimming, Note-Taking, and Source Integration**

#### **Skimming Techniques**

- **Purpose**: To quickly eliminate non-relevant, overly superficial, or excessively advanced sources.
- **Skimming Books**: Review the preface/introduction, acknowledgments, table of contents, chapter endnotes, index, and representative text paragraphs.
- **Skimming Articles**: Review the abstract, introduction, reference list, section headings, and select paragraphs.

#### **Recording Bibliographic Information**

- **For Books**: Record author, title, publisher, place of publication, year, and call number/URL.
- **For Articles**: Record author, article title, periodical title, volume, issue number, publication date, page range, and URL/DOI.

#### **Three Main Note-Taking Methods**

1. **Paraphrasing**:
    - Restating information completely in your own words and sentence structure.
    - Copying as few as 2–3 consecutive words from the original without quotation marks constitutes improper paraphrasing.
    - Requires careful comprehension, rewriting, adding a descriptive title, and recording full citation details.
2. **Quoting**:
    - Preserving exact phrasing when the original is exceptionally well-expressed or provides authoritative weight.
    - Direct quotes should be kept brief (rarely exceeding 2–3 sentences).
    - Use brackets `[]` to insert explanatory clarifications and ellipses `...` (or `[...]` in MLA style) to denote omitted text.
3. **Summarizing**:
    - Condensing a passage in your own words while retaining its core message.
    - Key technical document summaries include letters of transmittal, abstracts (technical overview), executive summaries (nontechnical managerial overview), and conclusions.
    - Process: Read carefully, underline key ideas, combine paraphrased points, and ensure factual proportions match the original.

### **4. Documenting Sources & Citation Styles**

#### **Functions of Documentation**

- **Ethical Obligation**: Acknowledges intellectual debt and prevents plagiarism.
- **Establishes Credibility**: Demonstrates that the work is grounded in existing scientific research.
- **Reader Utility**: Enables readers to locate original sources for further study.

#### **What Must Be Documented**

- Any direct quotation (even a few words).
- Any paraphrased idea, concept, or opinion (unless it is common, general knowledge).
- Any graphic, chart, or visual borrowed from print or digital sources.

#### **Three Primary Citation Styles**

- **APA Style (American Psychological Association)**:
    - Uses an author-date parenthetical system in text: `(Author, Year, p. X)`.
    - Includes an alphabetical **Reference List** at the end of the document.
- **IEEE Style (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers)**:
    - Uses bracketed sequential numbers in text: `,`.
    - References are numbered in the order of their first appearance in a numbered list at the end.
- **MLA Style (Modern Language Association)**:
    - Uses an author-page parenthetical system in text: `(Author Page)`.
    - Includes an alphabetical **Works Cited** list at the end.

### **5. Ethics, Academic Integrity, and Data Representation**

#### **Core Ethical Principles in Technical Writing**

- **Honesty**: Refraining from exaggerating claims or misrepresenting technical data.
- **Accuracy**: Ensuring all facts, figures, and calculations are error-free.
- **Clarity**: Writing unambiguous text to eliminate misinterpretation, particularly in safety instructions.
- **Completeness**: Providing all necessary context and information, not just data that reflects favorably on a product or hypothesis.

#### **Academic Integrity & Data Representation**

- **Five Fundamental Values**: Built on honesty, trust, fairness, respect, and responsibility.
- **Data Reporting**: Technical writers must present all experimental results, including unexpected or unfavorable data points. Omitting low test results (e.g., material stress breaking points) can result in real-world structural or product failures.
- **Consequences of Unethical Reporting**: Project failures, severe safety risks to human life, ruined professional reputation, legal liability, and job termination.

#### **Intellectual Property (IP) & Confidentiality**

- **Copyright**: Grants creators exclusive rights to original expressions, written reports, software code, illustrations, and diagrams. It does **not** protect underlying ideas, concepts, general facts, or raw scientific data.
- **Other IP Forms**: Patents (inventions/processes), Trademarks (brand names/logos), and Trade Secrets (confidential business formulas/client lists).
- **Confidentiality**: Professional obligation to safeguard non-public information, client data, and items covered under Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs) on a "need-to-know" basis.

### **6. Version Control & Technical Collaboration Tools**

#### **Version Control Systems (VCS)**

- **Definition**: A system that records file changes over time, acting as a full project revision history.
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Repository (Repo)**: Central database storing all files and version history.
    - **Commit**: A descriptive snapshot "save point" of the project at a specific time.
    - **Branching**: Creating isolated parallel versions to develop features without affecting the main codebase.
    - **Merging**: Combining changes from a feature branch back into the main branch.
    - **Conflict**: Occurs when overlapping line changes exist between branches, requiring manual resolution.
- **Engineering Best Practices**: Make frequent, logical commits with clear messages; use branches for new tasks; resolve merge conflicts promptly; and back up to remote platforms like GitHub or GitLab.

#### **Collaborative Writing Tool Stack**

- **Co-Authoring / Documentation Tools**: Confluence, Google Docs (real-time co-authoring, commenting, revision tracking).
- **Communication Platforms**: Slack and Microsoft Teams (channel-based chat), Zoom/Google Meet (video calls), and Email (formal asynchronous notices).
- **Project Management Systems**: Trello (kanban workflow), Jira (bug tracking, sprint management for tech documentation), and Asana.
- **Version Control Platforms**: Git, GitHub, GitLab, and SVN.
